Section 01

Why this matters for you

Recruiting for US healthcare and IT roles is not just about matching a resume to a job description. Every call, email, and scheduling decision carries cultural context. Getting it right builds trust in seconds. Getting it wrong costs you the candidate or the client.

01

Scheduling that actually works

Knowing time zones and daylight saving changes means you stop double-booking calls or emailing a Chicago hiring manager at what feels like midnight to them.

02

Instant credibility on calls

Pronouncing "Arkansas" or "Oregon" correctly, and knowing a candidate's state at a glance, signals that you know their world, not just their resume.

03

Smoother candidate conversations

Casual small talk about weather, weekend plans, or a local team is often how US candidates warm up before getting to business. Being ready for it builds rapport fast.

04

Fewer compliance surprises

Licensing boards, state labor rules, and even holidays differ by state. Understanding that government layers exist helps you ask the right questions instead of assuming one rule fits all fifty states.

05

Professional, not robotic, emails

US business email has its own rhythm: direct subject lines, brief bodies, clear asks. Matching that tone makes you read as a peer, not an outsourced vendor.

06

A genuine edge over other vendors

Clients and candidates remember recruiters who "get" their world. This is a quiet differentiator that compounds across every submission and every call.

Section 02

The flag, and the fundamentals

Before the map and the accents, a quick anatomy lesson. The US flag itself is a conversation starter and a quiet piece of shared knowledge that every American recognizes instantly.

  • 0113 stripes — represent the original thirteen colonies that became the first states.
  • 0250 stars — one for every current state, arranged in nine offset rows on the blue canton.
  • 03Nicknamed "Old Glory" and "The Stars and Stripes" — both are safe, common references in conversation.
  • 04Red, white and blue appear everywhere in US corporate and civic life, especially around July 4th (Independence Day).
  • 05Recruiter tip: candidates often mention their state flag or state pride casually. Knowing state names and nicknames helps you follow along.

Section 07

Time zones & daylight saving time

The continental US spans four zones, plus Alaska and Hawaii. This is the single most common scheduling mistake new recruiters make, so keep this section handy.

Eastern
UTC-5 / UTC-4 (DST)
New York, Florida, Georgia, Virginia, Ohio
Central
UTC-6 / UTC-5 (DST)
Texas, Illinois, Louisiana, Tennessee
Mountain
UTC-7 / UTC-6 (DST)
Colorado, Utah, Montana, New Mexico
Pacific
UTC-8 / UTC-7 (DST)
California, Washington, Oregon, Nevada
DST

Most of the US "springs forward" one hour in mid-March and "falls back" in early November. During this window the gap between IST and every US zone shifts by an hour — always double check a scheduling tool's converted time rather than relying on memory. Two exceptions worth knowing: Arizona does not observe daylight saving time, and Hawaii never observes it either, so their offset from India stays constant all year.

Section 09

How US government bodies differ

You do not need a civics degree, just enough to understand why rules can differ so much from one state to the next, and why a candidate's licensing or paperwork can look different depending on where they live.

Federal

The National Layer

Sets country-wide law: immigration, federal taxes, and national labor standards. Applies the same in all 50 states.

  • The President & Congress
  • Federal agencies (e.g. Dept. of Labor)
  • Same rules nationwide
State

Where Most Variation Lives

Each state has its own governor, legislature, and licensing boards. This is why a nursing license or a background check requirement can differ between Texas and New York.

  • Governor & state legislature
  • Professional licensing boards
  • State-specific labor & tax rules
Local

City & County Level

Cities and counties can add their own rules on top of state law, like local minimum wage or municipal permits. Rarely relevant day-to-day, but good to know it exists.

  • Mayors & city councils
  • County offices
  • Local ordinances

Recruiter-relevant note: licensing compacts

For healthcare roles, many states now participate in the Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC), which lets a nurse hold one multistate license valid across all participating states, instead of applying state by state. Not every state participates, so this is always worth confirming per candidate rather than assuming.

Section 10

Corporate culture & etiquette

US workplace culture tends to be direct, time-conscious, and informal in tone while still being professional in substance. Here is what that looks like in practice.

⏱

Time & Punctuality

Do
  • Join calls 1-2 minutes early
  • Stick to the scheduled end time
  • Send a calendar invite with a clear agenda
Don't
  • Run 10+ minutes over without checking in
  • Reschedule last-minute without a heads up
  • Assume flexible "IST time" norms apply
💬

Communication Style

Do
  • Get to the point in the first sentence
  • Say "I don't know, let me check" when true
  • Use first names once introduced
Don't
  • Over-explain before the actual ask
  • Read directness as rudeness
  • Over-use formal titles after introductions
🤝

Hierarchy & Feedback

Do
  • Speak up if you disagree, respectfully
  • Expect direct, specific feedback
  • Treat feedback as coaching, not criticism
Don't
  • Wait to be asked before raising a concern
  • Take directness personally
  • Assume seniority means someone can't be questioned
👔

Meetings & Calls

Do
  • Open with a brief, friendly line, then business
  • Mute when not speaking on group calls
  • Confirm next steps out loud before hanging up
Don't
  • Skip small talk entirely and jump straight in
  • Multitask visibly on video
  • Let a call end without a clear action item
Section 11

Social etiquette & everyday life

The small, human details, calls, food, entertainment, and email, that make conversations feel natural instead of scripted.

📞

Calls & General Talking

Small talk is a warm-up, not a formality. A quick "How's your week going?" or a comment on weather or weekend plans is normal before getting into business. Keep it brief, genuine, and let the other person set the tone.

"Please do the needful"→"Could you take care of this?"
"Revert back to me"→"Get back to me" / "Reply when you can"
"I will do it tomorrow itself"→"I'll do it tomorrow"
✉️

Email Etiquette

US business email is short and scannable. A clear subject line, a two-to-four sentence body, and one specific ask work better than a long, formal letter.

Do
  • Use a specific subject line
  • Bold or bullet the actual ask
  • Sign off with your name and role
Don't
  • Write dense paragraphs with no breaks
  • Bury the ask in the third paragraph
  • Use "Dear Sir/Madam" for a named contact
🍔

Food & Small Talk Topics

Safe, easy conversation starters: weekend plans, the local weather, sports (American football, basketball), and popular food like barbecue, tacos, or diner breakfasts. Thanksgiving (late November) and the Fourth of July are two dates almost every American will mention casually around that time of year.

📺

Shows & Pop Culture

You don't need to watch everything, just recognize names when they come up: major sports leagues (NFL, NBA), late-night talk shows, and big annual moments like the Super Bowl in February and awards season in the spring. A simple "Oh I've heard of that!" keeps the conversation moving naturally.
